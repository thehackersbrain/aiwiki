Before 2020 there was no principled way to decide how large a model to train.
Kaplan et al. supplied one, and the form of their answer has never been
seriously challenged.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — smooth power laws over seven orders of magnitude">

$$
L(N) = \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N},
\qquad
L(D) = \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D},
\qquad
L(C) = \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{\alpha_C}
$$

</Eq>

with $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ and $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$. No thresholds, no
plateaus, no discontinuities — loss falls predictably and the exponents are
small, which is the fact that made scaling a strategy rather than a hope.

Their allocation result was that given extra compute, most of it should go to
parameters: $N \propto C^{0.73}$, $D \propto C^{0.27}$. GPT-3 was built to that
recommendation — 175B parameters on 300B tokens, fewer than two tokens per
parameter — and so was almost everything trained in the two years after.

<Note label="caveat">
Chinchilla later showed the true exponents are both about 0.5, meaning parameters
and data should grow together. A 70B model on 1.4T tokens beat GPT-3 at a quarter
of its size. The gap between the two answers is one of the most expensive errors
in the field's history, and it came from methodology rather than from the theory.
</Note>

### The three causes

Porian et al. reproduced both papers and isolated what differed. Three choices,
and each moves the exponent.

**The learning-rate schedule.** Kaplan et al. trained every model with a single
schedule set for their longest run. A [cosine schedule](/entries/learning-rate-schedules)
truncated early leaves the model mid-decay and undertrained, so every small-$D$
point was worse than it should have been — which makes data look less valuable
than it is. Chinchilla tuned the schedule to each run's length. This is the
largest of the three.

**Embedding parameters.** Kaplan et al. excluded them from $N$; Chinchilla
included them. At the small end of the sweep, embeddings are a large fraction of
the model, so the two conventions disagree most exactly where the fit is most
sensitive.

**Warmup.** Small models in the Kaplan sweep spent a significant fraction of
their brief training in warmup, again penalising short runs.

Correct all three and the Kaplan setup reproduces Chinchilla's exponent. The
disagreement was never about the science.

<Rule label="What survived" />

Quite a lot, and it is worth separating from what did not.

The power-law form itself, over seven orders of magnitude in compute — this is
the finding, and everything since is a refinement of the constants.

The irrelevance of architectural detail: within a wide band, depth, width and
aspect ratio move the loss far less than scale does. The result that made it
reasonable to stop redesigning transformers and start enlarging them.

The critical batch size, $B_{\text{crit}} \propto L^{-4.8}$ — a prediction of how
large a batch can be before extra examples stop buying anything, still the
starting point for that calculation.

<Rule label="The lesson that generalises" />

The failure was not in the model of scaling. It was that a hyperparameter held
fixed across the sweep became a confound, and the confound was correlated with
the variable being studied.

That is a hazard for any scaling study, and it is why
[methodology](/entries/scaling-law-methodology) is a topic in its own right.
Something has to be held fixed across a sweep, and the question of whether the
thing you fixed is neutral with respect to what you are measuring has no general
answer — only, as here, an expensive empirical one.
