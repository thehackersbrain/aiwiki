A floating-point number is a sign, an exponent and a mantissa. The exponent sets
how far apart the largest and smallest representable magnitudes are; the mantissa
sets how finely spaced the values are within that span. A format is a decision
about how to split a fixed bit budget between the two, and every such decision is
a bet on which kind of error the workload can tolerate.

| Format | Exponent | Mantissa | Max finite | Smallest normal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| fp32 | 8 | 23 | 3.4e38 | 1.2e−38 |
| tf32 | 8 | 10 | 3.4e38 | 1.2e−38 |
| bf16 | 8 | 7 | 3.4e38 | 1.2e−38 |
| fp16 | 5 | 10 | 65 504 | 6.1e−5 |
| fp8 e4m3 | 4 | 3 | 448 | 1.5e−2 |
| fp8 e5m2 | 5 | 2 | 57 344 | 6.1e−5 |

### Why bf16 displaced fp16

fp16 came first and has more precision. It also has a dynamic range that gradient
magnitudes routinely leave. A gradient of $10^{-8}$ is not small for a deep
network; in fp16 it is zero, and the update it should have produced never
happens. At the other end, an activation spike above 65 504 becomes infinity, and
one infinity propagates to a NaN loss within a step.

The mitigation was *loss scaling*: multiply the loss by a large constant before
the backward pass so that gradients land in fp16's representable band, then
divide it back out before the optimiser step. Dynamic loss scaling automates the
constant by doubling it periodically and halving it whenever the gradients
overflow — which means a training run that intermittently discards steps as a
matter of course.

<Note label="annotation">
bf16 deletes the whole apparatus. Same exponent as fp32, so anything fp32 can
hold bf16 can hold; the price is three mantissa bits, which for stochastic
gradient descent is noise on top of noise. No loss scaling, no skipped steps, and
casting to and from fp32 is a truncation.
</Note>

The precision loss is not free everywhere, and the exceptions are the reductions:
sums over the feature dimension in [normalisation](/entries/layer-normalisation),
the softmax denominator, the optimiser's second-moment accumulator. All of those
are kept in fp32 in every serious implementation, which is what "mixed precision"
actually means — the weights and activations are half precision, the accumulations
are not.

### fp8, and why it needs a scale

Four exponent bits reach 448. Activations in a large model exceed that routinely,
so fp8 is not a format one simply casts into — every tensor carries a scale
factor, and the value stored is $x / s$ with $s$ chosen so the tensor's maximum
lands near the top of the range.

The two variants split the work. **e4m3** has more mantissa and less range, and
holds weights and activations. **e5m2** has more range and less mantissa, and
holds gradients, which span more orders of magnitude and tolerate coarser
steps. Getting this backwards is a real and common mistake.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — per-tensor scaling, chosen from the observed maximum">

$$
\hat{x} = \mathrm{cast}_{\text{fp8}}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right),
\qquad
s = \frac{\max|x|}{F_{\max}}
$$

</Eq>

Because $\max|x|$ is not known until the tensor exists, production implementations
use *delayed scaling*: keep a short history of recent maxima, predict the next
one, and correct if the prediction was too low. Recomputing the scale from the
current tensor is exact and costs a full extra pass over it.

<Rule label="Implementation" />

<CodeFrame meta="python · torch">

```python
import torch
from torch import Tensor

FP8_E4M3_MAX = 448.0


def to_fp8(x: Tensor, amax: Tensor | None = None) -> tuple[Tensor, Tensor]:
    """Scale into range, then cast. Returns the tensor and its scale."""
    amax = x.abs().amax() if amax is None else amax
    # clamp: an all-zero tensor would otherwise divide by zero.
    scale = (amax / FP8_E4M3_MAX).clamp(min=1e-12)
    return (x / scale).to(torch.float8_e4m3fn), scale


def from_fp8(x: Tensor, scale: Tensor, dtype=torch.bfloat16) -> Tensor:
    return x.to(dtype) * scale
```

</CodeFrame>

The matmul is where the win is. An fp8 tensor-core matmul runs at roughly twice
the throughput of bf16 on Hopper and later, and — more importantly for serving —
halves the bytes moved, which is the actual bound. The accumulator inside the
kernel is still fp32; fp8 describes the operands, never the sum.

<Rule label="Below eight bits" />

fp4 (e2m1) has six representable magnitudes. Per-tensor scaling is hopeless at
that width, so the block moves to *micro-scaling*: a shared exponent per group of
16 or 32 elements, so the scale tracks local structure rather than the whole
tensor. Blackwell implements this in hardware as MXFP4 and NVFP4.

Whether four-bit *training* works is genuinely open. Four-bit inference is
settled — [GPTQ](/entries/gptq) and its successors are in production — and the
distinction is that inference quantises a finished weight once, against a
calibration set, while training has to keep gradients meaningful through a format
with six magnitudes in it.
