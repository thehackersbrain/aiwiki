A [reward model](/entries/reward-modelling) is correct near the responses it was
trained on and unconstrained elsewhere. Maximise it freely and the policy leaves
that region immediately, because the highest-scoring text under a proxy is
usually text the proxy has never seen.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — the objective every RLHF method optimises">

$$
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr]
\;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)
$$

</Eq>

$\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the [supervised fine-tuned](/entries/supervised-fine-tuning)
model — frozen, and the definition of "still a reasonable language model". $\beta$
sets how far the policy may travel from it.

### The closed form

Eq. 1 has an exact solution, which is worth knowing because two other entries
depend on it.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — the optimal policy, for any reward">

$$
\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
$$

</Eq>

The reference distribution reweighted by exponentiated reward. It cannot be
sampled from — $Z(x)$ sums over all sequences — which is why PPO exists. But it
can be *rearranged*: solve for $r$ in terms of $\pi^*$, substitute into the
Bradley–Terry likelihood, and the partition function cancels. That is
[DPO](/entries/direct-preference-optimisation), and it is why DPO has a $\beta$
even though it never computes a KL.

<Note label="annotation">
Reverse KL, not forward. $\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is mode-seeking: it
punishes the policy for putting mass where the reference puts none, and is
relaxed about dropping modes the reference had. That is why aligned models are
less diverse than their base models — the objective explicitly permits it.
</Note>

### Estimating it

The KL cannot be computed exactly over sequences, so it is estimated from samples,
and the choice of estimator matters more than it looks.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — three estimators of the same quantity">

$$
k_1 = \log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}
\qquad
k_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)^2
\qquad
k_3 = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi} - 1 - \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi}
$$

</Eq>

$k_1$ is unbiased and high-variance, and is negative about half the time — an
estimate of a non-negative quantity that is routinely negative is a poor
diagnostic even when its mean is right. $k_2$ is low-variance and biased. $k_3$
is unbiased *and* non-negative, being a Bregman divergence, and costs one extra
exponential. It is what current implementations use, and it is Schulman's blog
post rather than any paper that established it.

### Two places to apply it

**In the reward.** Subtract $\beta \log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ per token from the
reward before computing advantages. The KL then flows through the value function
and the advantage estimator like any other reward.

**In the loss.** Add the estimated KL as a separate term. GRPO does this, which
is cleaner to reason about since the penalty does not interact with the critic.

They are not equivalent — the first shapes credit assignment, the second does
not — and papers are frequently unclear about which they mean.

<Rule label="Implementation" />

<CodeFrame meta="python · torch">

```python
import torch
from torch import Tensor


def kl_k3(logp: Tensor, logp_ref: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    """Schulman's k3: unbiased and never negative. logp: [B, T] per-token."""
    ratio = logp_ref - logp                       # log(π_ref / π)
    return ratio.exp() - ratio - 1.0


def adaptive_beta(beta: float, kl: float, target: float = 6.0, horizon: int = 10_000) -> float:
    """Ziegler et al. §2.2: steer the coefficient towards a KL budget."""
    error = torch.clamp(torch.tensor(kl / target - 1.0), -0.2, 0.2).item()
    return beta * (1.0 + error / horizon)
```

</CodeFrame>

The adaptive controller is the underused half. A fixed $\beta$ means the realised
KL wanders as the policy improves — early in training the same coefficient buys a
much smaller step than late. Targeting a KL budget instead makes runs comparable
across reward models and across seeds, which a fixed coefficient does not.

<Rule label="Dropping it" />

The reasoning-model work is the first credible argument against. When the reward
is *verifiable* — a maths answer that is right or wrong, a test suite that passes
— it is not a proxy, so there is nothing to overoptimise into. Several
R1-style recipes set $\beta = 0$ and report that the long chains of thought the
model develops are precisely what a KL leash to the SFT policy would have
prevented.

The scope of that argument is narrow and worth keeping straight. It applies
exactly where the reward is ground truth. For anything scored by a learned model
or an [LLM judge](/entries/llm-as-judge), the proxy is back and so is the leash.
