[BatchNorm](/entries/batch-normalisation) normalises a feature across the
examples in a batch. LayerNorm turns the tensor ninety degrees and normalises an
example across its features. Everything else about the two is the same; that one
choice is the entire difference, and it is the reason one of them is in every
transformer and the other is not.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — statistics over d, per token">

$$
\mathrm{LN}(x) = g \odot \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}} + b,
\qquad
\mu = \frac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i,
\quad
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2
$$

</Eq>

Because $\mu$ and $\sigma$ depend only on the vector in front of it, the
operation is identical at training and inference, identical at batch size 1 and
1024, and identical for the fifth token of a sequence and the five-thousandth.
There is no running average to maintain and nothing to synchronise across
devices.

<Note label="annotation">
That independence is not a minor convenience. It is what makes autoregressive
decoding possible at all: a normalisation with cross-token statistics would make
the representation of token $i$ depend on tokens that have not been generated.
</Note>

### What the two terms do

The re-scaling is doing the work. The re-centring is mostly not, and this was
established well before RMSNorm made it official — Xu et al. found that
LayerNorm's benefit comes from how it shapes gradients rather than from the
forward statistics, and specifically from the derivative of the $1/\sigma$ term,
which projects out the component of the gradient along $x$.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — the gradient is projected, not merely scaled">

$$
\frac{\partial \mathrm{LN}}{\partial x} \cdot \delta
= \frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\delta - \bar{\delta}\,\mathbf{1} - \frac{\hat{x}^\top \delta}{d}\,\hat{x}\right)
$$

</Eq>

Two components are removed: the mean of the gradient, and its projection onto the
normalised activation. The second is the important one. It means a gradient step
cannot change the *magnitude* of $x$ — only its direction — which is why
normalised networks are insensitive to the scale of their weights and why the
learning rate does not need retuning as activations grow with depth.

<Rule label="Implementation" />

<CodeFrame meta="python · torch">

```python
import torch
from torch import Tensor, nn


class LayerNorm(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim: int, eps: float = 1e-5, bias: bool = True):
        super().__init__()
        self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(dim))
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(dim)) if bias else None
        self.eps = eps

    def forward(self, x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
        dtype = x.dtype
        x = x.float()                                  # statistics in fp32, always
        mu = x.mean(-1, keepdim=True)
        var = x.var(-1, keepdim=True, unbiased=False)  # biased: divide by d, not d-1
        x = (x - mu) * torch.rsqrt(var + self.eps)

        x = x.to(dtype) * self.weight
        return x if self.bias is None else x + self.bias
```

</CodeFrame>

`unbiased=False` is not a detail. Bessel's correction divides by $d-1$, every
reference implementation divides by $d$, and using the corrected estimator
produces weights that are subtly incompatible with every other stack — a
discrepancy of $d/(d-1)$ in the norm, small enough to train through and large
enough to shift outputs.

<Rule label="What gets dropped" />

Two simplifications are now standard, and both are subtractive.

The bias $b$ goes first. It is redundant with the bias of the linear layer that
follows, and removing it costs nothing measurable while removing a parameter
tensor per norm — most models since GPT-J ship without it.

Then the mean subtraction goes, which is [RMSNorm](/entries/rmsnorm): one
reduction pass instead of two, one term fewer in the backward pass, and no
measured cost. That LayerNorm survived a decade of being progressively hollowed
out without anyone finding a task where the removed parts mattered is the most
interesting fact about it.
