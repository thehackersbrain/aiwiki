Three papers in 2019 asked the same question — how much of LayerNorm is load
bearing — and gave three answers of increasing severity. [RMSNorm](/entries/rmsnorm)
dropped the mean and kept $d$ gains. ScaleNorm dropped the mean and kept one.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — direction preserved, magnitude learned">

$$
\mathrm{ScaleNorm}(x) = g\,\frac{x}{\|x\|_2}, \qquad g \in \mathbb{R}
$$

</Eq>

Every activation vector is projected onto a sphere of learned radius $g$. The
representation is now pure direction, and the only thing the layer can adjust is
how big the sphere is.

Set against RMSNorm the difference is one of arithmetic rather than kind:
$\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{d}\cdot\mathrm{RMS}(x)$, so the two normalisers differ by a
constant. What actually differs is the gain — a vector in one, a scalar in the
other.

<Note label="annotation">
That is the whole experiment, and it has a clean interpretation. A per-channel
gain lets the layer decide that dimension 1174 should be loud and 302 quiet; a
scalar cannot. The question is whether that per-channel freedom does anything,
and the answer turned out to be yes.
</Note>

### What the paper found, and what happened after

Nguyen & Salazar were working on low-resource translation, where they combined
ScaleNorm with pre-norm placement and warmup-free training, and reported gains
over post-norm LayerNorm baselines — plus a useful observation that the learned
$g$ ends up roughly constant across depth, which is a reason to think the
per-channel gains were not doing much.

They also introduced FixNorm — the same $\ell_2$ projection applied to the word
embeddings — which is the part of the paper that quietly survived: normalised
embeddings with a learned temperature are now standard in retrieval and
contrastive setups.

ScaleNorm itself did not survive. At the scale it was tested — translation
models of tens of millions of parameters — the single scalar was sufficient. At
the scale the field moved to, it was not: the per-channel gain absorbs
systematic differences in how much each residual-stream direction is used, and
those differences grow with width. RMSNorm is in every current model and
ScaleNorm is in none.

<Rule label="Implementation" />

<CodeFrame meta="python · torch">

```python
import math

import torch
from torch import Tensor, nn


class ScaleNorm(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim: int, eps: float = 1e-5):
        super().__init__()
        # √d: the expected ℓ₂ norm of a unit-variance vector in d dimensions,
        # so the layer starts out approximately norm-preserving.
        self.g = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(math.sqrt(dim)))
        self.eps = eps

    def forward(self, x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
        dtype = x.dtype
        n = x.float().norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True).clamp(min=self.eps)
        return (x.float() / n * self.g).to(dtype)
```

</CodeFrame>

`clamp` rather than the usual additive epsilon: the denominator here is a norm
rather than a variance, so it is already on the scale of the activations, and
adding $10^{-5}$ to a quantity of order 60 does nothing at all. The failure this
guards against is a genuinely zero vector, which happens more often than expected
after a ReLU-family activation.

<Rule label="Why it is worth keeping on the shelf" />

Read together, ScaleNorm and [DyT](/entries/dynamic-tanh) bracket the question
from both sides across six years. Both remove machinery from the normalisation
layer and report that the model does not mind; one of them was right about the
scale it tested and wrong about the scale that came next. That is the specific
risk to hold in mind when reading the second.
