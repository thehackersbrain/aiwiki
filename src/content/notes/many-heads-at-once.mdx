By the end of part 2 we had one attention operation: a similarity, a softmax, a
weighted sum. Part 3 looked at what its output matrix contains. This part is
about why nobody uses one of them.

### The averaging problem

Consider a single head reading the word *bank* in "she sat by the bank and
counted the notes". Two things want attention: *sat by* disambiguates the sense,
and *notes* is the object the eventual prediction depends on.

One softmax row has to produce one distribution. It can split its mass between
the two, and then the context vector is a blend — half of one signal and half of
the other, added into the same $d$ dimensions with no way to tell them apart
downstream.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — one row, one budget">

$$
c = \alpha_{\text{by}}\,v_{\text{by}} + \alpha_{\text{notes}}\,v_{\text{notes}},
\qquad \alpha_{\text{by}} + \alpha_{\text{notes}} \le 1
$$

</Eq>

This is not a subtlety I appreciated for a long time. The constraint is not that
attention cannot look at two places — it obviously can, and part 1 made a point
of that. It is that both retrievals land in *the same vector*, so they interfere,
and the network has no free parameters left with which to separate them.

### The standard fix

Run $h$ attention operations in parallel, each with its own projections, each on
a $d/h$-dimensional slice, and concatenate.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — h independent operations, one output">

$$
\mathrm{MHA}(x) = \bigl[\mathrm{head}_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, \mathrm{head}_h\bigr] W^O,
\qquad \mathrm{head}_i = \softmax\!\left(\frac{Q_iK_i^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_i
$$

</Eq>

Each head has its own $\alpha$, so each can attend somewhere different, and each
writes into its own slice of the concatenation. The interference is gone because
the outputs occupy disjoint dimensions until $W^O$ mixes them deliberately.

The parameter count is unchanged. $d_k = d/h$, so $h$ heads of width $d/h$ is the
same total projection size as one head of width $d$ — multi-head attention is
free relative to single-head, which is the detail that makes the argument
persuasive rather than merely appealing.

<Note label="annotation">
It took me a while to see that this is a <em>rearrangement</em> and not an
addition. The same weights, partitioned differently, buy the ability to hold
several retrievals apart. That is a surprisingly good deal, and it is the same
kind of trick as <a href="/entries/shared-and-fine-grained-experts">fine-grained
experts</a> — carve a fixed budget into more pieces and get combinatorially more
behaviour out of it.
</Note>

### What they actually learn

Here the tidy story ends, and I think the tidy story is taught for too long.

Michel et al. pruned heads from trained models one at a time. On most layers,
most heads could be removed with no measurable loss — many layers ran fine on a
single head. Voita et al. found the same and characterised the survivors:
positional heads that attend to a fixed offset, syntactic heads that follow
dependency relations, and rare-token heads. A small minority doing something
identifiable, and a majority that could be deleted.

That is not what "each head learns a different subspace" predicts. The prediction
is a division of labour; the observation is a handful of specialists and a lot of
redundancy.

<Note label="caveat">
Both papers prune <em>after</em> training. That the heads are removable at the
end does not mean they were useless during it — a redundant head may have been
providing gradient paths or exploring alternatives early on. "Prunable" and
"never mattered" are different claims and the experiments only support the first.
</Note>

### The reframing that helped me

Elhage et al.'s circuits framework gave me the version I now use. A head is not a
subspace specialist; it is a pair of maps.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — two circuits, and they are independent">

$$
\underbrace{W_Q^\top W_K}_{\text{QK: where to look}}
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{W_O W_V}_{\text{OV: what to write}}
$$

</Eq>

QK decides the attention pattern; OV decides what gets copied into the residual
stream when it attends. They compose only through the head, so a head is fully
described by "which positions does it read, and what does reading them write".

This is a better frame for two reasons. It explains why heads compose across
layers — an [induction head](/entries/induction-heads) is a head whose QK circuit
reads the output of a previous head's OV circuit, which the subspace story has no
vocabulary for. And it explains why so many heads look empty: a head with a
near-zero OV circuit attends to things and writes nothing, which is exactly what
a prunable head is.

### The direction it went afterwards

Everything since has reduced the head count on the *key and value* side while
keeping it on the query side. [Multi-query](/entries/multi-query-attention)
attention gives all heads one shared K and V;
[grouped-query](/entries/grouped-query-attention) attention shares them among
small groups.

The justification is KV-cache size, and it is entirely an inference argument —
but it is worth noticing that it works at all. If heads genuinely needed
independent subspaces, sharing the keys and values across eight of them should
hurt badly. It does not, and the redundancy above is presumably why.
