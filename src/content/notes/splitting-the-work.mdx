Parts 1 and 2 were about one device. This part adds more of them in the simplest
way available.

### The identity

The gradient of a sum is the sum of gradients. So the batch can be split, each
device can compute on its slice, and the results added.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — one collective per step">

$$
g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g_i
$$

</Eq>

Every rank starts identical, computes its own gradient, all-reduces, and steps.
Because they all apply the same averaged gradient to the same weights, they stay
identical — and nothing else needs synchronising. One tensor, once per step.

This is [gradient accumulation](/entries/gradient-accumulation) with the
micro-batches running concurrently instead of sequentially, which is a nice way
to see that they are the same idea against different resources: accumulation
spends time to save memory, data parallelism spends devices.

### Why the collective does not get worse

The thing I did not understand for an embarrassingly long time is why this
scales. Surely averaging across a thousand ranks costs a thousand times as much
as across two?

It does not, and the ring algorithm is why. Arrange the ranks in a circle, split
the gradient into $N$ chunks. Each rank sends one chunk to its neighbour and adds
what it receives; after $N-1$ steps every rank holds the complete sum for exactly
one chunk. Then circulate those around, another $N-1$ steps.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — bytes per rank, and the limit as N grows">

$$
2\,\frac{N-1}{N}\cdot S \;\longrightarrow\; 2S
$$

</Eq>

Twice the gradient size per rank, approaching a constant. The *latency* term
grows with $N$ — $2(N-1)$ hops — which is why small tensors are the problem and
why frameworks bucket hundreds of parameter tensors into a few large messages.

That flat bandwidth cost is the reason data parallelism is the outermost layer of
every distributed configuration. It is the only strategy whose communication does
not degrade with scale.

<Note label="annotation">
There is a second identity worth carrying: all-reduce = reduce-scatter +
all-gather. It looks like an implementation detail and it is the hinge of the
next section — if a rank only needs <em>its shard</em> of the summed gradient,
the second half can be skipped.
</Note>

### The waste

Every rank holds a full copy of the parameters, gradients and optimiser state.
That is part 1's 16 bytes per parameter, replicated $N$ times, to store one
model's worth of information.

For 1024 ranks that is 1023 redundant copies of the optimiser state — which part
1 established is two thirds of the total.

[ZeRO](/entries/zero-sharding) removes the redundancy in three stages. Shard the
optimiser state; then the gradients; then the parameters themselves, gathering
each layer's weights just before they are needed and releasing them after. Stage
3 is $16P/N$ per device rather than $16P$.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — where the extra communication comes from">

$$
\text{stage 1--2: unchanged bytes} \qquad
\text{stage 3: } +\ \text{all-gather of parameters, per layer}
$$

</Eq>

Stages 1 and 2 are close to free, because of the identity above — the all-reduce
was already going to happen and is simply decomposed. Stage 3 genuinely adds
traffic, since parameters must be gathered on the way forward and again on the
way back, and whether that is affordable is a question about the interconnect.

### The thing that has to stay identical

Everything above assumes the ranks remain in lockstep, and the ways that quietly
fails are worth listing because none of them raises an error.

**A different data order.** `DistributedSampler` needs `set_epoch` called every
epoch, or every rank uses the same permutation for the whole run. The model sees
one fixed ordering forever. No error, slightly worse model.

**A rank that skips a collective.** Any branch that differs between ranks — an
empty micro-batch, a conditional early exit — deadlocks the job at the next
barrier. The symptom is a hang with no output, which is a worse failure than a
crash because it looks like slowness.

**Clipping in the wrong place.** [Gradient clipping](/entries/gradient-clipping)
before the all-reduce clips a partial gradient against a global threshold, so
each rank scales differently and the averaged direction is wrong. Trains fine,
produces a worse model.

<Note label="caveat">
Every one of those degrades the run without failing it. This is the general
character of distributed bugs and the reason I now assert invariants rather than
inspect losses — a loss curve that looks normal is very weak evidence that a
thousand processes are doing the same thing.
</Note>

### Where it stops

Two limits. Data parallelism requires the model to fit on one device, which is
what ZeRO stage 3 and the model-splitting strategies address. And the effective
batch grows with the rank count, so past the critical batch size the extra
devices are buying gradient variance reduction that the optimiser cannot use.
