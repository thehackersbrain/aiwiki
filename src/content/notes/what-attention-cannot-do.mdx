Five parts building the operation up. This one is about its ceiling — not
efficiency, which part 4 dealt with, but things the arithmetic cannot express at
all. Each of the four below explains a behaviour I had previously filed as a
quirk.

### 1. The softmax cannot abstain

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — the constraint, and it is not negotiable">

$$
\sum_{j} A_{ij} = 1 \qquad \text{for every } i
$$

</Eq>

A row sums to one whether or not the head has anything to retrieve. There is no
"nothing relevant here" output; the mass has to land somewhere.

What models do about it is put the mass on a position whose value vector is close
to useless, and the earliest tokens are the natural candidates because every
query can see them. That is an [attention sink](/entries/attention-sinks), and
once you read it as *the operation has no abstain option*, three separate
observations collapse into one:

- The first token receives over half the attention mass in most heads of most
  layers, regardless of what that token is.
- Evicting it with a sliding window destroys generation — not gradually, but
  immediately.
- The same heads produce the extreme activation outliers that make those layers
  hard to quantise, because forcing large mass onto a useless position requires a
  large value to scale it away.

The proposed fix in both the streaming and the quantisation literature is the
same, which is a good sign the diagnosis is right: add a $1$ to the softmax
denominator, belonging to no position, so a row may sum to less than one.

<Note label="annotation">
I had read all three of those results separately and treated them as unrelated
curiosities. They are one constraint with three symptoms, and the constraint is
in eq. 1 — visible from the first day I saw the formula, if I had asked what
happens when a head has nothing to do.
</Note>

### 2. It has no idea what order anything is in

Attention is a sum over a set. Permute the inputs and every score, weight and
output permutes with them — the operation is exactly equivariant, and the notion
of "before" is absent from it.

This is not a deficiency to be apologised for; it is what makes attention
parallel, and part 1's whole argument was that parallelism is why it beat
recurrence. But it means position is bolted on from outside, and every scheme for
doing so is a compromise the operation itself does not constrain.

The consequence I did not expect is how much downstream difficulty this creates.
A model's context length is a property of its *position encoding*, not of
attention — which is why extending it is
[frequency arithmetic](/entries/rope-scaling) rather than retraining, and why
models fail past their training length in the specific way they do: the attention
still computes, and the positions it computes over are meaningless.

<Note label="aside">
A decoder is the partial exception. <a href="/entries/causal-masking">Causal
masking</a> is not symmetric, so position <em>i</em> sees a different number of
tokens than position <em>j</em>, and a model can recover its index from that
alone. Attention is permutation-invariant; masked attention is not, and that is
the whole content of the <a href="/entries/nope">NoPE</a> result.
</Note>

### 3. One layer is one step, and there is no loop

A transformer applies $L$ layers and stops. There is no `while`, no recursion, no
way to spend more steps on a harder input. Whatever the answer is, it must be
reachable in a fixed number of parallel steps.

Merrill and Sabharwal make this precise: a transformer with log-precision
arithmetic can only compute functions in the complexity class $\mathsf{TC}^0$ —
constant-depth threshold circuits. That excludes problems believed to require
sequential computation, and it excludes them *for any number of parameters*.
Making the model bigger does not help, because the bound is about depth.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — what the bound says, informally">

$$
\text{one forward pass} \;\subseteq\; \mathsf{TC}^0
\qquad \not\ni \qquad
\text{problems needing } \omega(1) \text{ sequential steps}
$$

</Eq>

The practical escape is the one the field actually took, and reading it this way
made it much less surprising to me. Chain of thought converts depth into
*length*: each generated token is a fresh forward pass conditioned on everything
written so far, so the scratchpad is the loop the architecture does not have.
[Test-time compute](/entries/test-time-compute-scaling) is not a prompting trick
that happens to help — it is the only available way to exceed a structural bound.

### 4. Bounded precision blurs long sequences

Hahn's earlier result is narrower and more concrete. Attention weights are
softmax outputs, and as the sequence grows, a fixed number of mantissa bits
cannot keep an unbounded number of distinct weights apart. Beyond some length,
the contribution of any single position to the average becomes indistinguishable
from zero.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — one position’s share, as n grows">

$$
\alpha_{ij} \sim \frac{1}{n} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{below representable resolution}
$$

</Eq>

Which is the underlying reason for a failure everyone has seen: retrieval from
very long context degrades in the middle. The usual explanation is training
distribution, and that is part of it — but there is also an arithmetic floor, and
a single token's weight competing against a hundred thousand others is near it.

### What this changed for me

I had been treating attention as a general-purpose mechanism with some
engineering rough edges. It is better understood as a specific operation with
four hard edges, and most of the interesting work of the last few years is
someone routing around one of them:

| Limit | The response |
| --- | --- |
| cannot abstain | pinned sinks, softmax-plus-one |
| no order | position encodings, and extending them |
| no loop | chain of thought, test-time compute |
| bounded precision | hybrid recall, retrieval |

That table is the most useful thing this series produced for me. When a new
method appears, asking which row it belongs to usually explains what it is for
faster than reading the abstract.
