We need $\alpha_{ti}$: how much output step $t$ should read from input position
$i$. Three requirements, all forced by what it is for.

1. It must depend on both sides — on what $t$ is looking for and on what $i$
   contains. A weight that ignores either is not doing selection.
2. The weights over $i$ must be non-negative and sum to one, or "weighted
   average" is a lie and the scale of $c_t$ drifts with sequence length.
3. It must be differentiable, or none of this trains.

The standard route is: compute an unnormalised score $e_{ti}$, then normalise.

<Eq caption="eq. 1 — score, then normalise" lead>

$$
\alpha_{ti} = \frac{\exp(e_{ti})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \exp(e_{tj})}
$$

</Eq>

<Note label="annotation">
Requirement 2 does not force a *softmax* specifically — dividing non-negative
scores by their sum would also do it. Softmax is chosen because exponentiation
makes the distribution sharpenable: a score gap of 3 becomes a ratio of 20.
</Note>

So the real question is $e_{ti}$.

### Attempt one: learn the comparison

Bahdanau's answer is to let a small network decide. Concatenate the decoder state
and the encoder state, push them through a tanh layer, project to a scalar.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — additive (Bahdanau) scoring">

$$
e_{ti} = v^{\top} \tanh\!\left( W_s s_{t-1} + W_h h_i \right)
$$

</Eq>

This works, and it has a real advantage: $s$ and $h$ need not have the same
dimension, because $W_s$ and $W_h$ map them into a shared space independently.

It also has a cost that took me a while to see as decisive. For each of the $n
\times m$ pairs, eq. 2 requires a tanh over a $d$-vector and a dot with $v$.
Those cannot be folded into a single matrix multiplication, because the
nonlinearity sits in the middle. You get $n \cdot m$ small operations rather than
one big one — which on a GPU is close to the worst possible shape for a
computation.

### Attempt two: just use the inner product

Drop the learned comparator. Two vectors in the same space already have a
similarity function.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — dot-product scoring">

$$
e_{ti} = s_{t-1}^{\top} h_i
$$

</Eq>

<Note label="in numbers">
For $n = m = 4096$ and $d = 64$: additive scoring is ~17 M tanh evaluations;
dot-product scoring is one $4096 \times 64 \times 4096$ matmul, which a modern
accelerator does in under a millisecond.
</Note>

Every score for every pair is now a single matrix product, $S = Q K^{\top}$. This
is the change that mattered — not because dot products are more expressive than a
learned comparator (they are less), but because the whole score matrix becomes
one GEMM.

The expressivity is bought back by projecting first. $Q = X W^Q$ and $K = X W^K$
are learned, so the model still chooses *which* subspace similarity is measured
in; it just does not get to choose the similarity function itself.

### The scale factor is not cosmetic

Here is the part I originally skipped, and skipping it is why I could not
reproduce a working implementation from memory.

Suppose $q$ and $k$ have independent components with mean 0 and variance 1. Then

<Eq caption="eq. 4 — variance grows with dimension">

$$
\Var\!\left[\, q^{\top} k \,\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \Var[\, q_i k_i \,] = d_k
$$

</Eq>

so the logits have standard deviation $\sqrt{d_k}$. At $d_k = 64$ that is 8, and
a gap of a few standard deviations between the largest logit and the rest sends
softmax to a one-hot vector.

That is bad not because the attention is sharp but because of what it does to the
gradient. The softmax Jacobian is $\diag(a) - a a^{\top}$; as $a$ approaches
one-hot, it approaches zero, and no gradient reaches $W^Q$ or $W^K$ at all. The
layer stops learning while looking perfectly healthy in the forward pass.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ restores unit variance and the problem goes away.

<Eq caption="eq. 5 — the whole thing, assembled" lead>

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \softmax\!\left( \frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V
$$

</Eq>

### Checking it myself

I did not believe the saturation argument until I plotted it, and it is a
five-line experiment worth doing rather than reading.

<CodeFrame meta="python · the saturation, measured">

```python
import torch

for d in (8, 64, 512):
    q = torch.randn(4096, d)
    k = torch.randn(4096, d)
    logits = q @ k.T

    for name, s in (("unscaled", logits), ("scaled", logits / d**0.5)):
        a = s.softmax(dim=-1)
        # Entropy in nats: log(4096) = 8.32 is uniform, 0 is one-hot.
        entropy = -(a * a.clamp_min(1e-12).log()).sum(-1).mean()
        print(f"d={d:4d} {name:9s} std={s.std():6.2f} entropy={entropy:5.2f}")
```

</CodeFrame>

At $d = 512$ the unscaled entropy collapses towards zero while the scaled version
stays near $\log n$. The scaled row is not sharp *yet* — sharpness is something
the model learns by growing the magnitude of $W^Q$ and $W^K$ during training. The
point of the scale factor is that it starts diffuse and lets training decide,
rather than starting saturated with no gradient to escape with.
