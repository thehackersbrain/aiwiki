This is the least finished thing here and I am publishing it anyway, because
writing it down has been more useful to me than getting it right would be.

The problem: far more is published than anyone can read, most results are
reported at a scale I do not have, and the abstract is written to make the method
sound general. I needed a filter.

### The four questions

I ask these before reading past the method section, and they decide how much
attention the rest gets.

**1. What was held fixed, and is it neutral?**

The [Kaplan](/entries/kaplan-scaling-laws) failure is the canonical example: a
learning-rate schedule held constant across a sweep, penalising exactly the runs
that varied the quantity being measured. The conclusion was wrong and nothing in
the paper was sloppy.

So: what is constant across the comparison, and could it plausibly interact with
the axis being varied? If the answer is "a hyperparameter tuned for one arm", the
result is about that tuning.

**2. Is the baseline tuned as hard as the method?**

Almost never. The authors spent months on their method and ran the baseline from
a config file. A new optimiser beating AdamW at the authors' AdamW settings is a
much weaker claim than it appears, and papers rarely state how much search each
arm received.

The tell is an ablation table where every row of the proposed method is tuned and
the baseline is one row.

**3. Does the improvement exceed the seed variance?**

Bouthillier et al.'s number has stayed with me: on standard benchmarks, seed and
data-order variation alone produces spreads comparable to the differences papers
routinely report as improvements. A single-run comparison with a 0.4-point gap is
not evidence of anything.

Three seeds with an interval is the minimum I now believe. Most papers report
one, and the omission is usually not mentioned.

**4. At what scale, and does the mechanism predict transfer?**

This is the question I get most wrong. A result at 100M may or may not hold at
70B, and the way to guess is not the size of the gap but whether the *mechanism*
depends on scale.

[ScaleNorm](/entries/scale-norm) is the cautionary case: a single scalar gain in
place of a per-channel one, correct at the width it was tested at, wrong at the
widths that came after — because the per-channel gain absorbs differences that
grow with width. The mechanism had a scale dependence and the paper had no reason
to notice.

<Note label="annotation">
The inverse also happens. Chinchilla's exponent was derived from models far
smaller than the ones it now governs, and it transferred, because the power-law
form is a claim about the shape rather than about any particular size. Small-scale
results transfer when the mechanism is scale-free and fail when it is not, and
telling which is the actual skill.
</Note>

### What I do with the answers

Roughly three buckets.

**Might be true for me.** Mechanism is scale-free, baseline looks fairly treated,
variance addressed. I will consider trying it, and I will run the baseline myself
first.

**True at their scale, unknown at mine.** Most things. I note it and wait for
someone else to report a replication at a size closer to mine.

**Probably an artefact.** Untuned baseline, single seed, a metric chosen after
the fact. I read the related-work section for the citations and stop.

### The parts I skip and the parts I read twice

Skipped: the introduction, the related work on first pass, and most of the
results section.

Read twice: the experimental setup, in full, including the appendix — this is
where question 1 is answered, usually inadvertently. And the limitations section
where there is an honest one, which is rarer than it should be and a strong
positive signal when present.

The best papers state their own confound. Porian et al. reconciling Kaplan and
Chinchilla, Besiroglu et al. failing to reproduce a fit from the paper's own
figures, Schaeffer et al. manufacturing emergence to show it can be manufactured —
these are the papers I trust most, and they share the property of trying to break
their own result.

<Rule label="Still open" />

Things I have not worked out, which is why this note is marked unfinished:

**How to weigh a result I cannot replicate at all.** Frontier-scale findings —
training dynamics at 400B, cluster-level behaviour — are unavailable to almost
everyone and are also the ones that matter most for understanding what is
happening. I have no procedure here beyond noting who reported it.

**When to trust a benchmark number.** Between
[contamination](/entries/contamination-detection),
[judge](/entries/llm-as-judge) bias and metric choice, I have gradually stopped
believing most reported evaluations, and I do not have a replacement — only a
vaguer sense of which sources have been careful before.

**How much to discount for incentive.** A paper from a lab shipping a product
with the technique in it is not thereby wrong. But it is not independent
evidence either, and I do not know what the correct adjustment is.

If I work any of these out I will revise this rather than write a second one.
