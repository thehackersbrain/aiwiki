Part 1 ended with two ways to ask a human what they want. This part takes the
first one — show me — as far as it goes.

### It is just more pre-training

Supervised fine-tuning has no new machinery. Same objective, same optimiser,
different data: prompt-and-response pairs instead of documents, with the loss
masked so it only applies to the response.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — the only difference is which tokens count">

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t \in \text{response}} \log p_\theta\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)
$$

</Eq>

That mask is the whole implementation and it is easy to get wrong. Include the
prompt tokens and you are training the model to generate questions as well as
answers, which degrades it in a way that produces a perfectly normal-looking loss
curve. I have made this mistake; the symptom was a model that would occasionally
continue past its answer into a plausible next user turn.

### It works alarmingly well

LIMA is the result that reset my expectations. A 65B base model, **one thousand**
carefully written examples, no reinforcement learning of any kind — and outputs
competitive with heavily post-trained models in human comparisons.

A thousand. Not a hundred thousand.

Their reading is the *superficial alignment hypothesis*: essentially all
capability arrives during pre-training, and post-training is teaching the model
which of its existing distributions to sample from. If that is right, then a
small number of examples suffices, because you are selecting a mode rather than
teaching a skill — and the evidence is consistent with it, since quality of
examples matters far more than quantity in every ablation I have seen.

<Note label="annotation">
This is the most practically useful thing in the series. If you have a specific
task, the correct first move is a few hundred excellent examples, not a
preference pipeline. I spent time building the latter for a problem the former
would have solved, on the assumption that more machinery meant better results.
</Note>

### The three ways it stops

**It cannot express a negative.** A demonstration says "produce this". It never
says "not that". If the model has a failure mode — hedging, over-refusing,
inventing citations — you can show it a thousand examples of not doing that, and
each one is only positive evidence for one alternative. The gradient never points
*away* from the failure, because the failure never appears in the data.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — what the two kinds of signal contain">

$$
\text{SFT: } \nabla \log p(y^{\text{good}})
\qquad\qquad
\text{preference: } \nabla \bigl[\log p(y^{w}) - \log p(y^{l})\bigr]
$$

</Eq>

The second term on the right is the one SFT structurally cannot have.

**The ceiling is the demonstrator.** The model is fitted to imitate the
distribution of responses in the data, so it converges towards the quality of
whoever wrote them. That is fine when the writers are excellent and expensive.
It is a hard bound when they are not — and for tasks where a good response is
hard to *produce* but easy to *recognise*, it is the wrong bound entirely.
Comparisons ask people for the easy judgement; demonstrations ask for the hard
one.

**Imitation is not the thing being imitated.** Gudibande et al. fine-tuned open
models on outputs from a much stronger one and got a result worth remembering:
human raters preferred the students, and targeted benchmarks showed no
improvement at all. The models had learned the *style* — the confident register,
the structure, the formatting — without the underlying capability.

<Note label="caveat">
That paper is the reason I distrust win-rate numbers reported alone. Style is
cheap to imitate and heavily rewarded by raters, so any evaluation that is purely
comparative will overstate what imitation buys. Pair it with something that has a
ground truth or you are measuring register.
</Note>

### Where it leaves us

SFT is not a rung on a ladder that gets discarded. Every pipeline in the rest of
this series *starts* from an SFT model — it is the reference policy that PPO
constrains towards, and the initialisation DPO improves. Skipping it does not
work.

What it cannot do is improve past its data, and it cannot be told what to avoid.
Both limits have the same shape: the signal is one-sided.

The fix is to ask the other question. Not "what should the answer be" but "which
of these two is better" — cheaper to collect, available for tasks nobody can
demonstrate well, and it carries a negative.
