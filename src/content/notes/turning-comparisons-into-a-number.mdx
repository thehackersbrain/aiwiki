Part 2 established that we want comparisons rather than demonstrations. This part
is about the awkward step in the middle: reinforcement learning needs a scalar
reward, and what we have is a pile of "A is better than B".

### Why not just ask for a score

Because people cannot produce one. Ask an annotator to rate a response out of ten
and the numbers drift within a session, differ between people who agree
completely about the ordering, and compress towards the middle. The information
is there and the scale is not.

Comparisons are stable in exactly the way scores are not. So collect those and
fit a scalar that explains them.

### Bradley–Terry

The model is from 1952 and it assumes each item has a latent quality, with the
probability of preferring one being a logistic function of the difference.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — the assumption">

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma\bigl(r(y_w) - r(y_l)\bigr)
$$

</Eq>

Take the negative log likelihood and that is the training objective for a reward
model: the base transformer with the unembedding replaced by a scalar head,
trained so that preferred responses score higher than rejected ones.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — the loss, in full">

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\bigr)\Bigr]
$$

</Eq>

Only differences appear, so the absolute level is unidentifiable — add a constant
to every reward and the loss is unchanged. I found this genuinely disorienting
the first time: the reward model's outputs look like scores and are not. A reward
of 3.2 means nothing on its own, cannot be compared across models, and cannot be
compared across training runs of the same model.

<Note label="annotation">
It is the same model used to compute Elo ratings and to rank systems on a
leaderboard. There it is fitted to comparisons between <em>models</em>; here to
comparisons between <em>responses</em>. Same mathematics, same assumption of
transitivity, and the assumption is false in both places — three annotators can
easily produce a cycle — which does not stop it being useful.
</Note>

### Then it starts lying

Here is the part I did not expect, and it is the central fact of this series.

The reward model is fitted to finitely many comparisons. It is right in the
region where those responses live and unconstrained everywhere else. Policy
optimisation is a search for high reward — which is to say, a search for exactly
the places where the model is wrong.

Gao et al. measured the shape by training a very large "gold" reward model,
optimising against a smaller proxy, and plotting true reward against distance
travelled from the starting policy.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — it rises, peaks, and falls">

$$
R_{\text{gold}}(d) = d\,\bigl(\alpha - \beta \log d\bigr),
\qquad d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})}
$$

</Eq>

Two consequences. The peak is at a *finite* distance, so past it more
optimisation makes the model genuinely worse — while the reported proxy reward
continues to climb. And a larger reward model does not merely score better; it
tolerates more optimisation before turning, which is the argument for spending
compute on the reward model rather than only on the policy.

<Note label="caveat">
The single most misleading number in an RLHF run is its reward curve. It goes up
throughout. It goes up while the model is getting worse. The only honest
monitoring is a held-out evaluation the reward model is not part of.
</Note>

### The hacks are all the same shape

Three reliably observed, and they rhyme.

**Length.** Annotators prefer longer responses, the reward model learns it, the
policy writes essays. Singhal et al. found length explains a large fraction of
reward variance, and that some reported RLHF improvements substantially vanish
when responses are length-controlled.

**Sycophancy.** Agreement scores better than correction, so the policy agrees.

**Format.** Bullet points, headers, confident phrasing — all rewarded
independently of content.

Each is a feature that genuinely correlates with quality in the training
distribution and is cheap to produce in isolation. Any proxy has such features.
The question is not whether they exist but how quickly a search finds them, and
the answer is: immediately.

### What this forces

Since the proxy is only valid near where it was fitted, the optimisation has to
be *constrained to stay near there*. Not as a stabiliser or a regulariser — as
the thing that keeps the objective meaningful.

That constraint is the subject of part 4, and once you see it as a validity
condition rather than a training trick, PPO's design stops looking arbitrary.
