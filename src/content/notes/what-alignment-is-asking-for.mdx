The first time I used a raw base model I assumed it was broken. I asked it a
question and it produced four more questions. I asked it to summarise something
and it appended a citation and a paragraph about a different paper.

It was working perfectly. I had misunderstood what it was for.

### The objective, taken literally

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — the entire training objective of a base model">

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_t \log p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}\bigr)
$$

</Eq>

Nothing in that says *be helpful*. It says: given this prefix, what is likely to
come next in the corpus this was fitted to. If the corpus is the internet, then
after a question the likely continuation is often another question — because
that is what a list of FAQs looks like, and a forum thread, and an exam paper.

The model is not refusing to answer. It is answering a different question, and
the different question is the only one it was ever asked.

<Note label="annotation">
This reframing did more for me than any amount of reading about RLHF. The gap is
not obedience. It is that <em>document continuation</em> and <em>respond to this
person</em> are distinct tasks, and pre-training only ever optimised the first.
</Note>

### What the model already has

It is worth being equally precise about what is *not* missing, because it decides
what post-training can be.

The knowledge is there. So is the syntax, the reasoning, the ability to write
code and translate and summarise — the abilities are all latent, and demonstrably
so, because the right prompt elicits them from a base model. GPT-3's few-shot
results are exactly this: given three worked examples, the likely continuation
becomes a fourth worked example, and the model produces one.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — what few-shot prompting is doing">

$$
p(\text{answer} \mid \text{examples} + \text{question}) \gg p(\text{answer} \mid \text{question})
$$

</Eq>

So the capability is present and the *addressing* is broken. You can reach it by
constructing a context where the desired behaviour is the likely continuation,
which is what prompt engineering is, and which is fragile and tedious.

Post-training makes the desired behaviour likely without the scaffolding. That is
all it is doing — and it explains a result that surprised me when I first met it:
alignment barely changes what a model knows. Benchmarks measuring knowledge move
very little between a base model and its instruct version. What moves is whether
you can get at it by asking.

### Three things we want, and they conflict

Askell et al.'s framing is the one that stuck: **helpful, honest, harmless.**
Useful mainly because they pull against each other.

Helpful and harmless conflict on any request where the useful answer is
dangerous. Helpful and honest conflict whenever the honest answer is "I don't
know" and the user wanted a number. Honest and harmless conflict when a true fact
is one someone should not be handed.

There is no setting where all three are maximised, so alignment is not
optimisation towards a target — it is a choice of trade-off, made by whoever
writes the guidelines and whoever labels the data. This is worth sitting with
before any of the mathematics, because every method in the rest of this series is
a way of *transmitting* a trade-off someone made, and none of them chooses it.

### Why the loss cannot be written down

Here is the difficulty that shapes everything after.

Suppose we accept the trade-off and just want to optimise for it. Write the loss
function for "helpful". Not an approximation — the actual function that takes a
prompt and a response and returns how helpful it was.

You cannot. There is no closed form, no reference implementation, no dataset of
labelled helpfulness that would generalise if there were. The target is defined
only by human judgement, and human judgement is available in one form: you can
show a person things and ask what they think.

That is the constraint the whole field is arranged around. Two ways to ask:

**Show me what you want.** Collect demonstrations and train on them. That is
[supervised fine-tuning](/entries/supervised-fine-tuning), and it is part 2.

**Tell me which of these is better.** Collect comparisons and fit something to
them. That is [reward modelling](/entries/reward-modelling), and it is part 3.

Every method in this series is one of those two, or a consequence of one of them.
