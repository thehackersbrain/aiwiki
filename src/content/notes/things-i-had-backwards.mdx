A wrong belief that you know is wrong costs nothing. This is about the other
kind.

### 1. Mixed precision halves memory

It increases weight memory. The bf16 copy is *additional* to an fp32 master, so
mixed-precision AdamW is 18 bytes per parameter against pure fp32's 16.

The saving is in activations and bandwidth, and it is a large net win — but I
sized a run on the assumption that halving the dtype halved the model's
footprint, and it did not load.

### 2. Attention weights show what the model is looking at

They show where a head put its softmax mass. Since [the softmax must sum to
one](/entries/attention-sinks), a head with nothing to retrieve still has to put
its mass somewhere, and it learns to dump it on the first token.

So more than half the mass in a typical attention map is a head saying *nothing
here*, rendered identically to a head saying *this, urgently*. I spent time
reading heatmaps before I understood that, and the interpretations I drew were
mostly noise.

### 3. BatchNorm works by reducing internal covariate shift

That is the original paper's explanation and it did not survive testing.
Santurkar et al. injected noise *after* the normalisation to deliberately worsen
the distribution shift and found training just as fast — then showed the actual
mechanism is that the reparameterisation smooths the loss landscape.

The lesson I took is not about BatchNorm. It is that a technique can work
reliably for years while its stated explanation is wrong, and the explanation
being wrong tells you nothing about whether the technique works.

### 4. Bigger models are the compute-optimal choice

[Kaplan et al.](/entries/kaplan-scaling-laws) said put the compute into
parameters. GPT-3 was built on it, and so was almost everything for two years.

Chinchilla showed the exponents are both about 0.5 — parameters and data should
scale together — and a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beat a 175B on 300B. The
discrepancy came from a learning-rate schedule held fixed across a sweep, which
penalised the short runs and made data look less valuable than it is.

<Note label="annotation">
This one changed how I read scaling papers generally. The failure was not in the
theory; it was that something held constant across the sweep was correlated with
the variable being measured. That is a hazard for every scaling study and it has
no general defence.
</Note>

### 5. Emergent abilities are phase transitions

Sharp jumps in benchmark curves looked like something real switching on.
[Schaeffer et al.](/entries/emergent-abilities) showed most of them are the
metric: exact-match on a five-token answer is $p^5$, and a smooth rise in $p$
produces a curve that looks flat and then vertical.

They manufacture the effect on demand in a vision model, which settles it. What
survives is the weaker and still awkward claim that loss does not predict
capability — but the phase-transition framing, which I had found genuinely
unsettling, is largely a plotting artefact.

### 6. The activation function matters

I read comparisons of ReLU, GELU, SiLU and Mish as though the choice were
consequential. [Shazeer's ablation](/entries/gelu) trained the whole matrix — each
activation with and without a gate — and the gated versions beat the ungated ones
clearly while the activations *within* each group were separated by noise.

Gating is the design decision. The function inside it is a detail. Llama uses
SiLU because Shazeer named the variant SwiGLU; had he named GEGLU we would all be
using GELU and nothing else would differ.

### 7. Gradient accumulation is approximate

I believed this because accumulated runs reached worse loss than the equivalent
large batch, and I attributed it to the method.

It is exact. The [implementations were
wrong](/entries/gradient-accumulation) — dividing each micro-batch's loss by the
number of accumulation steps rather than by the total token count, which weights
short sequences too heavily whenever the micro-batches are ragged. Several major
libraries had this until late 2024.

<Note label="caveat">
The general shape here is the one I most want to remember: I had an observation,
a plausible theoretical story, and they agreed. Both were wrong, and their
agreement is exactly what stopped me looking further.
</Note>

### 8. Attention replaced recurrence because it models sequences better

It replaced recurrence because it fits the hardware. An
[LSTM's](/entries/lstm-and-gru) recurrence is sequential, so training cannot
parallelise over the sequence — a length-1024 sequence is 1024 dependent kernel
launches. Attention computes every position at once, and on a machine that
rewards parallelism above all else, quadratic cost was worth paying.

Long-range modelling quality was never the deciding argument. The evidence is
that the whole modern recurrence branch — SSMs, RWKV, gated linear attention —
exists to recover the RNN's *inference* properties, and every one of them is
linear specifically so that a
[parallel scan](/entries/parallel-scan) can train it. If attention had won on
modelling grounds, that line of work would make no sense.

### The pattern

Six of the eight came from the same place: I accepted an explanation that was
offered alongside a result, without checking whether the explanation was the
thing that had been tested.

The result and the story about the result are different claims with different
evidence. Covariate shift, the parameter-scaling recommendation, emergence as a
phase transition, activation-function comparisons, accumulation being approximate
— in each case the measurement was fine and the narrative attached to it was
doing work it had not earned.

I have not found a way to be reliably careful about this. What I do now is
smaller: when a paper explains *why* something works, I try to notice whether the
experiments distinguish that explanation from the obvious alternative. Often they
do not, and the explanation is the authors' hypothesis presented in the register
of a finding.
