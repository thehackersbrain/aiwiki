Part 3 ended with a reward that is only valid near where it was fitted. The
objective has to say so.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — maximise reward, but not too far from home">

$$
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr]
\;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)
$$

</Eq>

$\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the [SFT model](/entries/supervised-fine-tuning) from part
2, frozen. $\beta$ decides how far the policy may travel.

I had read the KL term as a stabiliser — the kind of thing you add to stop
training exploding. It is not. Given part 3, it is a *validity condition*: it
keeps the policy inside the region where the reward model is still correlated
with what the reward model is a proxy for. Remove it and the optimisation is
maximising a function that is meaningless where it is heading.

### The closed form

Eq. 1 has an exact solution, and it is worth deriving because part 5 is entirely
a consequence of it.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — the optimal policy, for any reward">

$$
\pi^{*}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)
$$

</Eq>

The reference distribution, reweighted by exponentiated reward. It is clean, it
is exactly right, and it is useless — $Z(x)$ sums over every possible sequence,
so the policy cannot be normalised, cannot be sampled from, and cannot be
computed.

So we approximate it by gradient ascent. That is the entire reason reinforcement
learning is in the picture: not because language generation is naturally a
control problem, but because the objective we want has a solution we cannot
write down.

<Note label="annotation">
Notice also that it is <em>reverse</em> KL, not forward. Reverse KL is
mode-seeking: it punishes the policy for putting mass where the reference has
none, and tolerates dropping modes the reference had. Aligned models being less
diverse than their base models is not a side effect anyone failed to prevent —
the objective explicitly permits it.
</Note>

### What PPO adds, and why

Gradient ascent on eq. 1 needs samples from $\pi$, and $\pi$ changes with every
update. Naively that means generating fresh samples for every gradient step,
which is prohibitively expensive when generation is the slow part.

PPO reuses each batch of samples for several updates, which makes them
*off-policy* — drawn from an older version of the policy than the one being
updated. Correcting for that needs an importance ratio, and importance ratios
have unbounded variance, so PPO clips.

<Eq caption="eq. 3 — the clipped surrogate">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\min\bigl(\rho_t A_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,A_t\bigr)\Bigr],
\qquad
\rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t)}
$$

</Eq>

The clip removes the incentive to move any single token's probability very far in
one update. Combined with the KL term there are now *two* constraints on how far
things move, at different scales — the clip bounds per-update drift from the
previous policy, the KL bounds total drift from the SFT model. Conflating them
cost me an afternoon once; they are unrelated mechanisms that happen to look
similar.

### Four models

This is the part that shapes practice more than any equation.

| Model | Role | Trained |
| --- | --- | --- |
| policy | the thing being improved | yes |
| reference | the KL anchor | frozen |
| reward | scores completions | frozen |
| value | estimates the baseline for advantages | yes |

Four copies resident simultaneously, two of them receiving gradients. For a 70B
policy that is a serious amount of hardware, before any of the usual difficulties
of getting PPO to converge.

<Note label="caveat">
The value model is the piece most often glossed over and the one that causes the
most trouble. It has to estimate the expected return from a partial sequence,
starting from a random initialisation, while the policy it is estimating for is
changing underneath it. Early training is the value model being wrong, which
makes the advantages wrong, which makes the policy updates noise.
</Note>

### Estimating the KL badly

One small thing that took me longer than it should have. The KL is estimated from
samples, and the obvious estimator is bad.

<Eq caption="eq. 4 — three estimators of the same quantity">

$$
k_1 = \log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}
\qquad
k_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\log^2\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}
\qquad
k_3 = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi} - 1 - \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi}
$$

</Eq>

$k_1$ is unbiased and enormously noisy, and is negative roughly half the time —
an estimate of a provably non-negative quantity that keeps coming out negative
makes for a useless diagnostic even when its expectation is right. $k_3$ is
unbiased *and* non-negative, for one extra exponential. Use it. The reference for
this is a blog post rather than a paper, which is a small lesson of its own about
where the operational knowledge lives.

### Is any of it necessary

Ahmadian et al. asked the obvious question — PPO was designed for continuous
control with long episodes and dense rewards, and RLHF has a single terminal
reward on a full response. Much of PPO's machinery is for a setting that does not
apply.

They removed most of it and found a REINFORCE-style estimator with a simple
baseline matches or beats PPO on RLHF, at a fraction of the complexity. The value
model, the clipping, the advantage estimation — largely inherited rather than
required.

[GRPO](/entries/grpo) is the version of that argument that got adopted: sample
$k$ responses per prompt, use their mean reward as the baseline, delete the value
model. Three models instead of four, and it is what the reasoning models are
trained with.
