Backpropagation needs the forward pass's intermediates. That is a requirement of
the chain rule and not negotiable. What is negotiable is whether those
intermediates were *stored* or are *recreated on demand*.

### The √L result

Chen et al.'s version is elegant enough to derive in a paragraph. Divide $L$
layers into $k$ segments. Keep only the activation at each segment boundary — $k$
tensors. During the backward pass, when a segment is reached, rerun its forward
from the stored boundary to regenerate its interior.

<Eq lead caption="eq. 1 — memory and recompute, traded against k">

$$
M \sim k + \frac{L}{k}, \qquad \text{extra compute} \sim \frac{L}{k} \cdot \frac{L}{L} = 1 \text{ forward pass}
$$

</Eq>

Minimising the first over $k$ gives $k = \sqrt{L}$ and $M \sim \sqrt{L}$ — memory
sublinear in depth, for one extra forward pass. For an 80-layer model that is
memory proportional to 9 rather than 80.

<Note label="aside">
The idea is older than deep learning by two decades. Griewank's Revolve solves
exactly this problem — optimal checkpoint placement for reverse-mode
differentiation — for adjoint solvers in computational fluid dynamics, and the
optimal schedule for a known memory budget is a binomial recursion rather than
the even split everyone actually uses.
</Note>

### The number in practice

One extra forward pass sounds cheap. Put it in the [6ND
accounting](/entries/training-compute-accounting) and it is not.

<Eq caption="eq. 2 — the constant, with and without">

$$
\underbrace{2}_{\text{fwd}} + \underbrace{4}_{\text{bwd}} = 6
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
\underbrace{2 + 2}_{\text{fwd, twice}} + \underbrace{4}_{\text{bwd}} = 8
$$

</Eq>

Thirty-three percent more compute for the entire run. On a month-long job that is
ten days. It is a real price and it is routinely paid without being stated —
scaling-law plots are drawn in FLOPs and the checkpointing multiplier does not
appear anywhere in them.

### Why uniform recomputation is the wrong shape

Here is the observation that reframed this for me. The memory in a transformer
block is not evenly distributed, and neither is the cost of regenerating it.

Sort the tensors by *arithmetic intensity* — FLOPs to recompute, divided by bytes
stored.

| Tensor | Bytes | Recompute cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| attention scores | $5as^2b$ | one matmul |
| softmax output | $2as^2b$ | elementwise |
| dropout mask | $as^2b$ | free |
| QKV projections | $6sbh$ | a large matmul |
| FFN hidden | $8sbh$ | a large matmul |

The quadratic tensors — the large ones — are almost free to regenerate. The
linear tensors are matmul outputs, where recomputing means repeating the
expensive part.

So the right move is not "recompute the block". It is: recompute the attention
interior, keep the projections. Korthikanti et al. measured about 70% of full
checkpointing's saving at roughly 4% overhead instead of 36%.

<Note label="annotation">
A ninth of the cost for most of the benefit, purely from not applying the
technique uniformly. This is the general lesson I took from the memory branch:
almost every "trade X for Y" technique has a version that asks <em>which</em> X,
and the selective version is usually dramatically better than the blanket one.
</Note>

### And it composes badly with the free thing

The table above assumes the score matrices are stored. If you took part 1's
advice, they are not — [FlashAttention](/entries/flash-attention) already made
that trade, better, and for free.

Which means selective recomputation has much less left to work on than the
numbers suggest. Its three big targets are gone, and what remains is the linear
term where the exchange rate is poor.

This is the honest state of the technique in 2026: important historically,
important in stacks that cannot use fused attention, and considerably less
central than the papers make it sound if you are running a modern kernel.

### The third option

There is a route neither of these takes: keep the activations and store them in
fewer bits. The backward pass reads them once to compute a gradient that is
already a noisy minibatch estimate, so a little extra noise is tolerable —
[ActNN](/entries/activation-compression) makes this rigorous with stochastic
rounding, which keeps the gradient unbiased and preserves convergence guarantees.

It gets about twelve-fold compression for roughly 20% throughput. Sound, and
mostly unused — because recomputation is exact, is one function call, and after
FlashAttention there is not enough left on the table to justify custom kernels.

I like it as an entry anyway. It is a good example of a technique that is
correct, well-motivated, and was overtaken by something that solved the same
problem from a different direction.
